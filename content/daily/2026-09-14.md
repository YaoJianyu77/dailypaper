## Latest papers 1 — From Tokens to Layers: Redefining Stall-Free Scheduling for MoE Serving with Layered Prefill

**MLSys · 2026-05-18 · Gunjun Lee, Jiwon Kim, Jaiyoung Park, Younjoo Lee, Jung Ho Ahn.** [Official publication](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/c0f460c6d63599ea870ba9db63dc96a9-Abstract-Conference.html) · [Full paper](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/file/c0f460c6d63599ea870ba9db63dc96a9-Paper-Conference.pdf)

### 1. Paper in brief

Layered prefill changes how a serving engine shares GPUs between processing incoming prompts and generating tokens for existing requests. Conventional chunked prefill divides prompts into token blocks, keeping individual decoding delays manageable. For mixture-of-experts (MoE) models, however, each block can activate nearly every expert while providing too few tokens to reuse its weights efficiently. The proposed scheduler instead advances the prompt through contiguous groups of transformer layers, alongside decoding, one group per iteration. This reduces repeated expert loading without requiring separate prefill GPUs. On GPT-OSS-20B with two H100s and arXiv summarization traffic at 2.1 requests/s, mean time to first token falls from 3.00 to 0.87 seconds. The contribution is a scheduling design whose benefits depend on MoE sparsity and streaming latency constraints (pp. 6–10).

### 2. Problem and core insight

The engine accepts prompts and returns autoregressive token streams. It seeks high serving capacity while meeting service-level objectives (SLOs) for time to first token (TTFT), including queueing, and time between tokens (TBT). Prefill builds the key-value (KV) cache; decoding repeatedly consumes and extends it. Both stages share accelerators (pp. 1–3).

The first insight is that token sparsity does not guarantee batch sparsity. Qwen3-30B-A3B selects eight of 128 experts per token, yet sufficiently large hybrid prefill/decode batches activate almost all experts. Small chunks then repeat those weight reads with little reuse per expert. The authors call this sparsity erosion (p. 5).

The second insight is that limiting prefill depth can control iteration work while retaining many tokens per active layer. Consequently, scheduling can preserve expert reuse and spread prompt processing across decode iterations. The benefit is strongest when long prompts require many conventional chunks and decode batches remain relatively small (pp. 5–6).

### 3. How the method works

Incoming prompts enter the prefill queue, receive a layer-group configuration, advance through the model over successive iterations, and finally produce their first token before joining ordinary decoding. Concurrent short prompts can be merged into one batch (pp. 6–7).

The model is divided into Nlg contiguous groups. Every iteration processes existing decode requests through every group, but at most one designated group also processes prefill. A prompt advances from group one to group two on the next iteration, completing after Nlg iterations. Its prefill computation traverses each layer once, avoiding the repeated expert reads caused by small token chunks. Decoding still requires its own weight accesses; the design does not eliminate those (p. 6).

For prompt length L, the implementation chooses Nlg = max(1, ceil(L/512)), approximately matching the work granularity of the 512-token baseline. An 8,192-token prompt therefore uses 16 groups. More groups reduce interference per iteration but delay first-token completion. Very long inputs can combine large token chunks, such as 8,192 tokens, with layered scheduling within each chunk (p. 7).

Implementation uses vLLM, FlashAttention-3, custom CUDA kernels, torch.compile, and CUDA Graphs. The main experiments distribute model computation through tensor parallelism across NVLink-connected GPUs (p. 7). Interpretation. Resuming prefill requires retaining intermediate hidden states between groups. Their memory footprint, allocation policy, and scheduling overhead are Not specified in the paper.

### 5. Experimental evidence

The principal setup uses two 80-GB H100s, bfloat16 weights and activations, Qwen3-30B-A3B, and GPT-OSS-20B. ShareGPT averages 2,340 input/438 output tokens; arXiv averages 9,194/231. Arrivals follow a Poisson process. The baseline uses 512-token chunked prefill. TTFT limits are 5 seconds for ShareGPT and 10 for arXiv; TBT limits are 125 ms for Qwen and 100 ms for GPT. A request succeeds only if TTFT and every subsequent TBT satisfy their limits (pp. 7–8).

First, latency improves at fixed load. Table 8 shows GPT mean TTFT decreasing from 3.00 to 0.87 seconds at 2.1 requests/s on arXiv, a calculated 71% reduction. Mean TBT improves from 25.1 to 18.2 ms, although p99 TBT rises from 30.6 to 32.3 ms. Figure 4 indicates that capacity gains mainly reflect better TTFT attainment; both schedulers generally already satisfy TBT (pp. 9–10).

Second, a fixed trace of 100 Qwen requests reduces counted expert-weight loads from 35.6 to 21.7 TB on arXiv, versus 28.5 to 25.1 TB on ShareGPT. The reported reductions are 39% and 12%, supporting the predicted advantage for longer prompts (Table 7, p. 9).

Third, Qwen's reported energy falls from 56.6 to 51.7 mJ/token at equal load, and to 44.2 at 1.6 rather than 1.3 requests/s. The maximum 22% saving therefore combines scheduling improvement with higher utilization. Energy comes from differences in cumulative NVML GPU counters, without a component-level energy breakdown (pp. 8–10).

### 6. Contributions and limitations

The contribution combines an MoE reuse diagnosis, a layer-based scheduler, and implementation in an existing serving stack. It changes execution organization without proposing model retraining (pp. 6–7).

Stall-free operation depends on configuration: Table 11 reports p99 TBT of 138 ms with two groups, exceeding Qwen's 125-ms limit. Relaxed latency constraints let larger conventional chunks close the gap. Dense Qwen3-8B actually regresses, with mean TTFT of 1.45 versus 0.955 seconds on one H100 (p. 11).

Broader tests reach eight H100s and larger models, but Table 9 includes TBT regressions despite the surrounding general claim of comparable or improved TBT. Interpretation. Multi-node scaling and adaptive grouping remain insufficiently established. Software version pins and confidence intervals are Not specified in the paper. Table 8's output-token wording conflicts with the prompt-plus-output denominator explicitly defined on pages 6 and 8; its absolute energy values should retain that qualification.

### 7. Final assessment

Interpretation. The paper convincingly demonstrates that scheduling granularity can undermine MoE weight reuse, and that layer boundaries offer a useful alternative for colocated serving. Its strongest evidence connects lower expert traffic with improved first-token latency under fixed load. Its weakest aspects are incomplete implementation overhead accounting and inconsistent energy labeling. The conclusions are most reliable for sparse MoE models with long prompts, small decode batches, and tight streaming constraints; dense models and relaxed latency targets offer much less justification.

## Latest papers 2 — CRAFT: Fine-Grained Cost-Aware Expert Replication For Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Serving

**MLSys · 2026-05-18 · Adrian Zhao, Zhenkun Cai, Zhenyu Song, Lingfan Yu, Haozheng Fan, Jun Wu, Yida Wang, Nandita Vijaykumar.** [Official publication](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/3a7f9e485845dac27423375c934cb4db-Abstract-Conference.html) · [Full paper (1/2)](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/file/3a7f9e485845dac27423375c934cb4db-Paper-Conference.pdf) · [Full paper (2/2)](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/file/3a7f9e485845dac27423375c934cb4db-Supplemental-Conference.pdf)

### 1. Paper in brief

CRAFT improves serving of mixture-of-experts (MoE) models by deciding where additional expert copies justify their GPU memory cost. Expert parallelism distributes experts across GPUs, but uneven token routing overloads devices hosting popular experts. Replication spreads that work; uniform replication also consumes memory needed by the attention key/value (KV) cache, reducing concurrent requests. CRAFT estimates replication benefits separately for each layer, allocates a shared replica budget, and balances replica storage across devices. In SGLang experiments on 64 A100 GPUs, the authors report 1.14× average throughput improvement, up to 1.2×, over the evaluated Expert Parallelism Load Balancer (EPLB) replication baseline. Its contribution is a practical allocation framework that preserves most load-balancing benefits while leaving more memory for serving concurrency (pp. 1–2, 8).

### 2. Problem and core insight

An MoE router selects a small subset of expert networks for each token. Expert parallelism dispatches tokens to their hosting GPUs and combines the resulting outputs. The slowest loaded GPU delays the collective, while uneven traffic creates congestion. Placement can pair popular and unpopular experts, but cannot divide one overwhelmingly popular expert’s workload without replication (p. 3).

Two observations drive CRAFT. First, replication benefit differs sharply across layers: already balanced layers need few copies, while strongly skewed layers need more. Second, gains saturate; further copies consume KV-cache memory without removing much additional imbalance (pp. 4–6). Consequently, CRAFT maximizes estimated aggregate balancedness gain under a replica budget. Balancedness means average GPU token load divided by maximum GPU token load. This is a proxy for serving performance, whose true objective also depends on available concurrency.

### 3. How the method works

Recorded expert loads → replay-based benefit estimation → budgeted layer allocation → capacity assignment and expert placement → a deployable replication plan (pp. 6–7).

The estimator receives token counts indexed by batch, layer, and expert. It constructs candidate placements using aggregate loads, then replays individual batches to estimate GPU balancedness. Positive replica counts follow a base-two progression up to the GPU count; placement without replication supplies the reference. The output is a matrix of estimated benefit for each layer and candidate count. Replay approximates each expert’s token load as evenly divided among its copies (Algorithm 1, combined p. 13).

For D GPUs, the budget is r = R × D additional copies, where R counts replicas per GPU across the model. A multiple-choice knapsack formulation selects one candidate count, including zero, per layer to maximize summed benefit within that budget. Dynamic programming provides the solution; Appendix A.2 states time complexity O(LCK) and space O(LC), for L layers, capacity dimension C, and K candidate counts (p. 13). Optimality concerns this discretized benefit model.

Next, capacity assignment gives additional slots to GPUs with the fewest total experts, spreading ties across nodes. This equalizes total replica storage while limiting within-layer capacity differences. Greedy placement then assigns heavily loaded experts to lightly loaded devices subject to those capacities (pp. 7, 14).

Inference retains the model’s routing and expert computation. For workload drift, CRAFT can replace the planner in periodic rebalancing; the serving framework handles asynchronous weight relocation. Benefit estimation takes approximately ten seconds in the reported setup and can run on CPU alongside inference (pp. 8, 10).

### 5. Experimental evidence

The setup uses AWS p4de.24xlarge nodes, each with eight 80-GB A100 GPUs, NVLink internally, and Elastic Fabric Adapter between nodes; CUDA 12.8, NCCL 2.26.2, and SGLang 0.4.8. DeepSeek-R1-671B and Kimi-K2-1000B use bfloat16 and top-eight routing. Workloads comprise German and Japanese/Chinese FinePDFs, Lambada, and RedPajama’s arXiv split, with 4,096-token inputs and 256-token outputs. Profiling samples are excluded from evaluation (pp. 4, 8).

Serving combines data parallelism, tensor-parallel attention, expert parallelism, and mixed chunked prefill capped at 4,096 tokens. Batches vary with arrivals and KV capacity. BASE uses EPLB placement without replication; EPLB adds one replica per layer per GPU; CRA8 uses eight replicas per GPU across all layers. Poisson arrivals produce throughput–time-to-first-token (TTFT) curves; goodput is their saturation knee, before prolonged queuing (p. 8).

On eight nodes, CRA8 improves goodput over EPLB by 1.15× on average for DeepSeek, up to 1.2×, and 1.12× for Kimi, up to 1.17×. It uses respectively 1/7.25 and 1/7.5 as many replicas (p. 8, Figure 8).

Below saturation, CRA8 reduces TTFT versus BASE by 29% on average, up to 58%; EPLB achieves 30% and 59%. This supports comparable prefill balancing with less replication (p. 8).

On six-node Kimi deployments, EPLB reduces KV capacity by 75% and averages 46% lower goodput than BASE. CRA8 reduces KV capacity by 6% and achieves 1.14× BASE goodput (p. 10). Component profiling attributes gains to reduced dispatch congestion and combine stalls, with little change in aggregate expert computation (p. 12).

### 6. Contributions and limitations

The contribution combines layer-specific resource allocation with storage-balanced placement. It requires neither retraining nor modified expert selection. Interpretation. Its strongest evidence concerns long-input, memory-constrained MoE serving; short-input or decode-dominated workloads may benefit less.

The supplement materially limits reproducibility: the released core artifact substitutes a relative-benefit threshold heuristic for the budgeted knapsack allocator and evaluates trace replay rather than reproducing distributed serving throughput (pp. 17–18). Automatic budget selection should therefore be distinguished from the fixed CRA8 evaluation.

Workload-shift tests use synthetic dataset mixtures and primarily measure balancedness (pp. 11, 15–16). Interpretation. They do not establish tail-latency guarantees during production weight migration. A numerical service-level threshold for identifying goodput: Not specified in the paper.

### 7. Final assessment

Interpretation. CRAFT demonstrates that layer-specific replication can improve serving capacity by preserving KV-cache memory while retaining useful load balance. Its strongest evidence combines distributed goodput measurements and replica-budget sweeps. Uncertainty remains about transferring its balancedness proxy and automatic allocation to other hardware, workloads, and latency targets. This is primarily a systems allocation contribution, with a narrower public artifact than the full serving evaluation.

## Latest papers 3 — Beyond the Buzz: A Pragmatic Take on Inference Disaggregation

**MLSys · 2026-05-18 · Tiyasa Mitra, Ritika Borkar, Nidhi Bhatia, Shivam Raj, Hongkuan Zhou, Yan Ru Pei, Vishwanath Venkatesan, Kyle Kranen, Ramon Matas, Dheevatsa Mudigere, Ritchie Zhao, Maximilian Golub, Arpan Dutta, Suresh Nambi, Sailaja Madduri, Dharmesh Jani, Brian Pharris, Itay Neeman, Bita Darvish Rouhani.** [Official publication](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/hash/d49cee5f3a79d97d719df255689d83d7-Abstract-Conference.html) · [Full paper (1/2)](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/file/d49cee5f3a79d97d719df255689d83d7-Paper-Conference.pdf) · [Full paper (2/2)](https://proceedings.mlsys.org/paper_files/paper/2026/file/d49cee5f3a79d97d719df255689d83d7-Supplemental-Conference.pdf)

### 1. Paper in brief

Prefill–decode disaggregation separates prompt processing from token generation so each phase can use different GPU partitioning, batching, and capacity. This paper studies when that flexibility improves inference efficiency. Co-located execution shares weights efficiently but couples two competing latency objectives; separating phases introduces cache transfers and resource-balancing problems. The authors explore throughput–interactivity trade-offs with a proprietary simulator and supplement them with NVIDIA Dynamo deployments. Benefits depend strongly on model size, prompt/output lengths, latency targets, and available interconnects. In an H200 prototype, Figure 12 reports 18.64 requests/s of goodput for Dynamo Planner versus 12.27 for the strongest plotted static configuration, although the prose claims a different improvement. The central contribution is deployment guidance: independently optimize phase mappings, then continuously match their processing rates (pp. 3–8).

### 2. Problem and core insight

Inputs are a model, hardware configuration, request arrival rate, input sequence length (ISL), output sequence length (OSL), and latency targets. The objective is high output-token throughput per total deployed GPU at a chosen interactivity, measured as tokens/s/user. First-token latency (FTL) measures prompt processing through the first token; token-to-token latency (TTL) measures subsequent generation. Interactivity equals 1/TTL. Appendix A defines service-level agreements using median FTL and TTL (pp. 3–4, 16).

Two observations drive the design. First, prefill and decode favor different mappings: prefill benefits from efficient parallel computation, while increasingly interactive decoding needs smaller batches and more communication or memory bandwidth. Separating them removes the compromise imposed by one shared mapping. Second, independently efficient pools can still waste resources when their request rates differ. Their replica counts must therefore change with workload and latency targets; one fixed prefill:decode ratio cannot cover the full trade-off curve (pp. 5–8).

### 3. How the method works

Model and traffic specifications enter a configuration sweep, which produces feasible phase mappings, matches their capacities, and returns throughput–interactivity frontiers. Tensor parallelism splits tensor computation, expert parallelism distributes mixture-of-experts components, and pipeline parallelism divides layers among GPUs. The simulator explores these choices and batch sizes separately for each phase. It selects the highest-throughput prefill configuration satisfying FTL, then combines it with candidate decode configurations using integer rate matching. Configurations exceeding ten seconds FTL are excluded (pp. 3–4, Algorithms 1–2).

Chunked pipeline parallelism divides a prompt into chunks. Each chunk uses preceding chunks’ key–value (KV) attention cache, while earlier layers process newer chunks concurrently with later layers processing older chunks. It reduces reliance on tensor-parallel collectives. Equations 1–2 attribute its communication advantage to transfers across pipeline boundaries instead of repeated all-reductions across layers (p. 5).

At runtime, prompts enter prefill workers; their KV state moves to decode workers, which generate output tokens. NVIDIA Inference Transfer Library supplies asynchronous, nonblocking transfers so computation can continue. Cache layout and transferring only missing cached blocks affect memory efficiency and bandwidth. The simulation assumes layerwise transfers overlap subsequent computation (pp. 4, 9–10).

Dynamo Planner first profiles candidate engines, then models prefill performance against ISL and decode performance against active cache usage and context length. Runtime monitoring adjusts replica counts; forecasting anticipates startup delays, and moving-average corrections compensate for profiling errors, queueing, and cache reuse. This adds profiling and control complexity; numerical controller overhead is Not specified in the paper (pp. 7–8).

### 5. Experimental evidence

The main exploration models Blackwell systems with FP4 precision, including GPU execution, memory, NVLink, Ethernet, and communication collectives. Baselines include co-location with and without chunked-prefill piggybacking. Most curves are normalized; they establish relative trade-offs rather than absolute serving capacity (pp. 2–3).

First, disaggregation’s advantage grows across Llama 8B, 70B, and 405B at ISL 4,096 and OSL 256. DeepSeek-R1 traffic sweeps similarly favor longer prompts relative to outputs. Figure 7 even places co-location above disaggregation for 8B near the highest-throughput end. These simulations support conditional benefits from broader mapping choices, not universal superiority (pp. 6–7, Figures 7–8).

Second, the H200 prototype uses DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B, synthetic ISL 3K, OSL 300, arrival rates 5–45 requests/s, and FTL/TTL targets of 200/10 ms. Associated profiling uses SGLang within Dynamo. Figure 12 reports Planner goodput of 18.64 requests/s and 3.96 requests/s/GPU. The strongest plotted static configuration, two TP1 prefill and two TP1 decode instances, achieves 12.27 and 3.07 respectively. Calculated ratios are 1.52× and 1.29×; the text’s 2× comparison is inconsistent with these plotted values. Against three TP1 prefill instances and one TP1 decode instance, plotted ratios are approximately 8.25× and 6.95× (p. 8).

Third, supplemental Figure 17 compares simulated dynamic traffic against fixed ISL 4,096/OSL 512 for Llama-3.1-70B. Their normalized frontiers closely track one another, supporting the median-length approximation for this example without establishing general tail-latency fidelity (p. 16).

### 6. Contributions and limitations

The contribution combines a configuration-space study, rate-matching methodology, and practical orchestration guidance. Its simulated gains assume sufficient requests and GPUs to fill balanced pools, generally without shared-prefix reuse. KV-transfer bandwidth estimates assume transfers large enough to saturate links and negligible transfer-latency terms; replicated cache shards require careful accounting (pp. 4, 9).

Interpretation. Reproducibility is limited by the proprietary simulator and normalized results. Quantitative simulator validation errors, prototype software versions, and confidence intervals are Not specified in the paper. The small-model prototype supports adaptive provisioning, but does not directly validate the large-model simulation sweep. Forecasting, mapping selection, and scaling are not separately ablated, so their individual contributions remain uncertain.

### 7. Final assessment

Interpretation. The strongest contribution is explaining why phase-specific mapping and capacity matching must be optimized together. The simulations provide useful conditional guidance, and the prototype demonstrates measurable benefits from adaptive orchestration. The weakest point is quantitative auditability: proprietary modeling, normalized curves, and Figure 12’s conflicting prose limit confidence in headline magnitudes. Conclusions are most trustworthy as deployment principles for adequately provisioned systems with characterized traffic and efficient KV transfer.

# Clear Research Trends in Today's Papers

## Match scheduling and allocation to layer-specific behavior

Interpretation. Today's MoE papers suggest that uniform resource policies miss important differences in expert activity. From Tokens to Layers retains more tokens within each active prefill layer group to reduce repeated expert-weight loading; CRAFT allocates replicas according to each layer's estimated balancing benefit instead of replicating uniformly (Layered Prefill, pp. 5–7; CRAFT, pp. 4–7). The shared direction is to make layer structure explicit in serving decisions. The unresolved trade-off is between local efficiency and end-to-end capacity: finer prefill grouping can delay first-token completion, while additional replicas consume KV-cache memory. Neither improved weight reuse nor improved load balance alone guarantees better serving performance.

Supporting papers: From Tokens to Layers: Redefining Stall-Free Scheduling for MoE Serving with Layered Prefill; CRAFT: Fine-Grained Cost-Aware Expert Replication For Efficient Mixture-of-Experts Serving.

## Optimize prefill–decode coordination for the workload and latency target

Interpretation. From Tokens to Layers and Beyond the Buzz address the same conflict: prompt processing and token generation favor different execution choices but must jointly satisfy serving latency targets. Layered Prefill coordinates them on shared GPUs by advancing prefill through layer groups; Beyond the Buzz independently configures separate phase pools and adjusts their capacity ratio (Layered Prefill, pp. 6–7; Beyond the Buzz, pp. 5–8). Together they support workload-dependent coordination as a design direction. The unresolved trade-off is how much separation justifies its costs: shared execution retains resource reuse but permits interference, while disaggregation adds KV transfers and capacity-balancing requirements. Dense-model regressions in Layered Prefill and small-model simulation results in Beyond the Buzz reinforce that neither approach establishes a universally preferable deployment.

Supporting papers: From Tokens to Layers: Redefining Stall-Free Scheduling for MoE Serving with Layered Prefill; Beyond the Buzz: A Pragmatic Take on Inference Disaggregation.
